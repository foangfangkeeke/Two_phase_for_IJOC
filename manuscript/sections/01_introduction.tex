\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Electrifying bus fleets requires coordinated planning of charging infrastructure, battery capacity, and the use of limited charging resources under uncertain future service requirements.
Wireless charging technologies expand the set of charging opportunities by allowing buses to receive energy at selected stops or road links during regular service.
This capability turns infrastructure planning from a stand-alone siting decision into a joint planning problem linking wireless facility deployment, route-level battery configuration, and the use of existing plug-in chargers.

Wireless charging facilities are long-lived assets, whereas transit service plans may change with future demand and procurement planning.
A layout that is attractive for one future service plan may perform poorly under another because route frequencies, trip chains, active-fleet requirements, and charging-resource use may change.
The infrastructure decision must therefore be made before the future service plan is known, whereas route-level battery configurations and operational decisions can adapt after a service-plan scenario is realized.

The central planning trade-off is shaped by the interaction among wireless infrastructure, battery capacity, and finite plug-in charging capacity.
More in-route wireless charging can reduce battery requirements on some routes and lower their residual demand for plug-in charging.
At the same time, facility placement changes when and where that residual demand occurs.
Because plug-in chargers are limited shared resources, concurrent charging requirements couple route-level decisions that would otherwise be separable.

We capture this timing and coupling structure with a two-stage stochastic mixed-integer planning model.
The first stage selects stationary and dynamic wireless charging facilities.
The second stage selects route-level battery configurations and, conditional on those selections, determines representative-day charging allocation and energy management for the realized service-plan scenario.
The resulting recourse problems contain discrete battery choices, plug-in charger-occupancy decisions, and shared plug-in capacity constraints.
Consequently, both the deterministic equivalent and the classical integer L-shaped decomposition become difficult to scale as the network and scenario set grow.

To solve this model, we develop a reformulation-enabled integer L-shaped method.
The method differs from a standard integer L-shaped decomposition by changing what recourse information is exposed to the master and by separating inexpensive lower-bound generation from exact recourse verification.
This design preserves exactness when the cut loop converges and creates the algorithmic components evaluated in the computational study.

The paper makes three contributions.
First, it identifies a layered two-stage stochastic mixed-integer structure in which here-and-now facility decisions interact with scenario-dependent discrete configurations and shared-resource operational recourse.
The wireless-charging application provides a concrete instantiation in which conditioning on battery choices reveals route-local operations, limited plug-in capacity couples routes, and wireless facilities and batteries form a joint operational resource order.
Second, it develops an exact reformulation-enabled integer L-shaped algorithm that combines scenario-dependent decision variables lifting, a two-phase relaxation-verification scheme, and monotonicity-strengthened cuts for mixed-integer recourse.
Third, it provides comprehensive computational evidence through an extensive algorithmic ablation study and systematic analyses of performance with respect to network size, scenario count, and the gap between the Phase 1 lower-bounding relaxation and the exact Phase 2 recourse evaluation.

The experiments use wireless-charging planning instances constructed from Singapore bus-service data for routes operated by Tower Transit.
They examine whether the model produces interpretable planning decisions, how the algorithmic components affect network-scale and scenario-scale performance, and how the complete method responds as shared plug-in capacity makes the Phase 1 relaxation more or less aligned with exact verification.
