\section{Computational Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

In this section, we conduct computational experiments to validate the proposed solution algorithms for the DWC planning problem.
The benchmark uses 28 Tower Transit bus routes in western Singapore, whose geographic coverage is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-map}.
The processed R28 network has 493 stations and 624 directed links.
All monetary values reported in the experiments are expressed in USD per representative day.
The algorithms are implemented in C++ and run on a computer with an Intel Core i5-14600K processor and 32 GB of RAM.
COPT 8.0.7 is used to solve the linear and mixed-integer programs.
Our code and data are available in the accompanying GitHub repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/foangfangkeeke/Two_phase_for_IJOC}.}

\subsection{Efficiency of the Proposed Algorithm}
\label{subsec:overall-performance}

An instance code has the form R-S-F-C, with the numbers following the four letters giving the route count, scenario count, candidate DWC link count, and chargers per existing plug-in location.
R3 and R12 are nested subsets of the 28-route network.
Each scenario specifies a timetable, bus duties, and the active fleet for one service plan on the same physical network.
The plans include a base case and service-frequency increases or decreases across the network, on routes serving a selected terminal, or on several individual routes.
Within each fixed route and scenario set, candidate DWC links are ranked by aggregate scheduled traversal time, so the F10, F20, and F50 sets are nested.
Figure~\ref{fig:station-visits} illustrates the variation in scheduled visits to the five existing plug-in stations across the R28-S40 service plans.
The total number of visits ranges from 2,491 to 3,309 per scenario, compared with 2,763 in the base plan.
Bt Batok and Jurong East have the two highest visit counts in every plan, and the counts vary across plans at all five stations.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/chapter5_station_visits.png}
\caption{Scheduled visits to the five existing plug-in stations across the R28-S40 service plans.}
\label{fig:station-visits}
\end{figure}

The main comparison combines the nested R3, R12, and R28 route sets with the F10, F20, and F50 DWC candidate sets, yielding nine S5-C4 instances.
The comparison includes two baselines (EF and ILS), two ablation variants (Lifted ILS and TPILS), and the proposed R-TPILS.
Relative to ILS, Lifted ILS uses an equivalent reformulation that lifts scenario-dependent battery variables into the master.
TPILS incorporates the two-phase framework, and R-TPILS further adds repair-enhanced monotone feasibility cuts.
Table~\ref{tab:method-components} summarizes these variants, and Table~\ref{tab:main-performance} reports their original-problem bounds, computation times, and stopping outcomes.

The performance gap between the two-phase methods and the remaining methods widens as the route set grows (Table~\ref{tab:main-performance}).
All five methods reach the 1\% target on R3, although Lifted ILS, TPILS, and R-TPILS finish sooner than EF and ILS.
At R12, ILS times out on every instance and EF runs out of memory at F50.
Lifted ILS still reaches the target, but requires 374--1,417 seconds, compared with 13--22 seconds for TPILS and R-TPILS.

The R28 cases separate the methods by whether they can verify a feasible solution within the limit.
EF reaches the memory limit, and neither ILS nor Lifted ILS reports a verified UB.
TPILS reaches the target on F10 and F50, while R-TPILS does so on all three instances.

\subsection{Algorithmic analysis}
\label{subsec:mechanism-analysis}

Following the overall comparison in Table~\ref{tab:main-performance}, we examine the roles of the algorithmic components in more detail.
The analysis covers the energy-budget inequalities, lower-bound progress in Phase 1, and convergence and repair in Phase 2.

\subsubsection{Energy-budget screening}
\label{subsec:vi-analysis}

The energy-budget inequalities reject master candidates that lack enough energy to cover a duty before the scenario subproblems are evaluated.
We assess how this screening affects Lifted ILS by comparing runs with and without VI on the same instances (Table~\ref{tab:vi-ablation}).

VI enables Lifted ILS to reach the 1\% target on R12 in 1,237.1 seconds, while its no-VI counterpart times out without a verified UB (Table~\ref{tab:vi-ablation}).
The lower-bound trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:vi-progress} show how the difference develops during the search.
After the first master solve, the VI version has a much higher LB, as the inequalities can screen out low-cost master candidates that violate the energy requirements.
For the no-VI version, the LB increases slowly over most of the run.
The final LB (2,743.81) of the no-VI version is slightly higher than the first recorded LB (2,679.47) of the VI version.
Evaluating the no-VI master candidates against the inequalities shows that all 20 would have been screened out before scenario evaluation.
A similar pattern occurs on the more challenging R28-S5-F50-C4 instance, where all four master candidates visited without VI violate the inequalities.

\subsubsection{Lower-bound progress in Phase 1}
\label{subsec:phase-one-analysis}

Phase 1 is shared by TPILS and R-TPILS.
It removes the shared plug-in capacity constraints and eliminates charger-occupancy variables, yielding independent route LPs that provide a valid lower bound for the original problem.
We assess how quickly the lower bound improves using the retrospective gap $g_t^{\mathrm{ret}}=100(UB^{\mathrm{ref}}-LB_t^R)/UB^{\mathrm{ref}}$, where $UB^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the best available UB for the same instance.

Figure~\ref{fig:l2p-progress} shows that the early Phase 1 iterations account for most of the lower bound improvement on the R28-S5-C4 instances.
After the first master solve, the retrospective gaps are 64.265\%, 65.518\%, and 67.450\% for F10, F20, and F50, respectively.
After the fourth master solve, they fall to 0.626\%, 1.178\%, and 0.751\% in 9.6, 10.2, and 10.9 seconds.
F10 and F20 terminate shortly thereafter, whereas F50 requires 162 additional iterations to meet the Phase 1 stopping criterion.
Even in the latter case, Phase 1 ends after 210.4 seconds, less than 3\% of the 7,200-second time limit.

\subsubsection{Repair in Phase 2}
\label{subsec:phase-two-analysis}

R-TPILS uses repair to strengthen monotone feasibility cuts in Phase 2.
When an exact scenario subproblem proves a master candidate infeasible, the repair search increases battery capacities and adds DWC facilities to identify an anchor that remains infeasible after these increases.
The cut generated from this anchor excludes a larger portion of the infeasible region.
In this process, the repair search may also find a verified feasible solution and thereby establish a UB.

Table~\ref{tab:repair-comparison} evaluates these effects across the eight R28 instances.
R-TPILS obtains a verified UB on all eight instances and reaches the target gap on six, whereas TPILS obtains a UB and reaches the target gap on only two.
When TPILS also obtains a UB, repair either reduces the time to the first UB or is not needed.
R-TPILS continues to obtain verified UBs as the number of scenarios increases from 10 to 40.
Over these instances, the share of Phase 2 time spent on repair rises from 27.6\% to 37.6\%, and S40 finishes slightly above the target gap at 1.080\%.
Thus, one of the main computational contributions of repair is to recover verified feasible solutions early in Phase 2.

Figure~\ref{fig:repair-phase2} traces this tradeoff in two representative cases.
On F10 (short for R28-S5-F10-C4), repair reduces the time to the first incumbent but does not accelerate convergence.
R-TPILS obtains an incumbent in its first iteration, yet repair consumes 20.0\% of its Phase 2 time and the method terminates later.
On C3 (short for R28-S5-F50-C3), the LB trajectories reveal a different role for repair.
R-TPILS initially trails TPILS, but the strengthened cuts allow it to overtake after 10 iterations.
When R-TPILS converges, its LB is 0.154\% higher than that of TPILS at the same elapsed time, and TPILS remains below this level even at the time limit.
The same run also shows the feasibility benefit of repair: R-TPILS obtains a UB in the first iteration and converges after 18 iterations, whereas TPILS completes 257 iterations without any incumbent.
Across these evaluations, the Hamming distance between successive R-TPILS master solutions has a median of 26, more than twice the median of 12 for TPILS.
The larger moves indicate that the repair-enhanced cuts redirect the master search, helping to achieve the later LB advantage.

\subsection{Planning outcomes and sensitivity}
\label{subsec:planning-implications}

In this section, we examine three factors affecting the final plans: (i) the availability of DWC, (ii) the battery unit cost, and (iii) the DWC construction cost.
The main analysis uses R28-S5-F50-C4, with the service-plan scenarios and existing plug-in capacity held fixed.
The resulting plans are evaluated in terms of solution costs, battery capacities, charging allocations, and DWC deployment.

\subsubsection{Planning effects of DWC}

To assess the cost implications of DWC, the no-DWC case prohibits all candidate DWC installations and reoptimizes the remaining decisions.
Table~\ref{tab:dwc-planning-costs} shows that the DWC-enabled plan is about 4\% less costly, primarily because the reduction in battery expenditure outweighs the added facility and charging costs.
With smaller batteries, buses rely more heavily on charging during service, when electricity prices are higher than those for overnight charging.
Daytime charging expenditure therefore rises substantially and more than offsets the reduction in overnight charging expenditure, so total charging expenditure increases.
The DWC-enabled plan also costs about 672 less than its no-DWC counterpart under the tighter C3 plug-in capacity.
Overall, DWC reduces total cost by substituting roadside infrastructure and daytime charging for onboard battery capacity.

The same resource substitution is reflected in the battery and charging decisions shown in Figure~\ref{fig:planning-adjustments}.
Across all vehicles and scenarios, the mean battery capacity decreases by about 23\%.
Nearly half of the route--scenario pairs select a smaller battery, while none selects a larger one.
Almost all reductions coincide with direct DWC use.
Route~41 illustrates the strongest route-level shift and uses DWC in every scenario.

Expected plug-in energy increases by about 10\%, with increases at most existing stations.
The DWC-enabled plan therefore combines wireless and plug-in charging rather than simply replacing the latter.
Together with the lower overnight-charging expenditure in Table~\ref{tab:dwc-planning-costs}, this pattern indicates a shift toward daytime charging while buses carry smaller batteries.

\subsubsection{Cost sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:planning-cost-sensitivity} and Table~\ref{tab:planning-cost-sensitivity} show that the battery unit cost has a pronounced effect on DWC deployment.
Across the tested levels, the cost difference increases from 36 to 1,600~USD/day and the number of selected links from 5 to 39, while the mean battery capacity in the DWC-enabled plan falls from 179 to 133~kWh.
Mean battery capacity decreases more slowly at higher battery cost levels even as DWC deployment continues to expand, indicating diminishing scope to replace additional onboard storage.
