\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}

Two streams of research inform this study: electric-bus charging facility location and decomposition methods for stochastic programs with mixed-integer recourse.

\subsection{Electric-bus charging facility location}
\label{subsec:lit-wireless-bus}

For a given bus service, larger batteries can reduce the need for daytime charging, while more charging opportunities at stations can allow buses to operate with smaller batteries \citep{perumal_review_2022}.
Daytime charging must fit around scheduled trips, making both travel to charging stations and waiting for a charger relevant to facility planning.
\citet{bazarnovi_locating_2025} jointly choose station locations and the number of chargers at each station under stochastic charging demand, balancing investment against the time costs of travel to stations, waiting, and charging.
By supplying energy as buses travel, DWC adds charging opportunities along a route.
The locations and lengths of equipped road segments affect how much energy a bus can receive and, in turn, the battery capacity needed to serve the route.
In a system with existing plug-in chargers at depots and terminals, \citet{li_planning_2024} jointly determine DWC deployment, route-level battery capacities, and charging schedules under time-of-use electricity prices.

The operating conditions that determine charging needs can be uncertain.
\citet{li_mixed_2021} use a two-stage stochastic model to locate refueling facilities and schedule a mixed bus fleet before the driving ranges of electric buses are known.
Additional services are deployed after the ranges are realized to cover scheduled trips left incomplete because of energy shortages.
Under uncertain travel times and energy consumption, \citet{wang_reliable_2024} use a two-stage robust model to determine the locations and lengths of DWC facilities and the battery capacities for each route.
En-route charging is adjusted after the uncertain conditions are realized.
The service plan itself may also be uncertain when facilities are deployed.
Service plans may differ in their scheduled trips and in how those trips are assigned to buses, changing which DWC-equipped links buses traverse and when they can charge at stations.

For a given service plan, buses on different routes may need to charge at the same station, where the number of chargers limits how many buses can charge at once.
Given the trips assigned to each bus, \citet{abdelwahed_opportunity_2020} optimize the timing and duration of charging subject to these limits.
Other studies jointly assign trips to buses and schedule charging, subject to station capacity limits \citep{gkiotsalitis_multi_depot_2023,devos_capacitated_2024,ricard_battery_2026}.
Even if a feasible charging schedule exists for each route separately, there may be no feasible schedule for all routes together because buses from different routes compete for the same chargers.

\subsection{Decomposition methods for stochastic integer programs}
\label{subsec:lit-stochastic-decomposition}

Benders decomposition separates an optimization problem into a master problem and subproblems, using cuts from the subproblems to refine the master.
In classical Benders decomposition, the subproblems are linear programs (LPs) whose dual solutions and rays provide optimality and feasibility cuts.
For two-stage stochastic programs with continuous linear recourse, the L-shaped method applies this decomposition across scenarios.
With integer recourse, an LP relaxation may underestimate the recourse cost or fail to detect infeasibility.
For problems with binary first-stage decisions and complete recourse, the integer L-shaped method uses integer recourse evaluations to construct cuts \citep{laporte_integer_1993}.
Logic-based Benders decomposition also accommodates integer subproblems, deriving cuts through problem-specific reasoning \citep{hooker_logic_2003}.
Nevertheless, limited information from cuts can lead to many iterations, while solving the subproblems at each iteration can be costly \citep{rahmaniani_review_2017}.

Reformulations can make recourse information available to the master before cuts are generated.
Retaining selected scenario subproblems in the master provides an explicit representation of their recourse decisions \citep{crainic_partial_2021,celik_twostep_2025}.
Moving individual scenario-dependent variables into the master follows the same principle, but can make the master large when there are many scenarios.
\citet{lai_strengthened_2025} exploit customer choices that coincide across scenarios to reduce this growth.
Even with a stronger master, evaluating recourse across many scenarios can make cut generation expensive.
Aggregating scenarios or sampling a subset can reduce the work involved \citep{ramirez_adaptive_2023,bertsimas_stochastic_2025}.
The aggregation can be refined using subproblem dual solutions, whereas sampled evaluations can be combined through dual averaging to construct valid cuts.

Problem structure can provide additional information for the master through valid inequalities and stronger cuts.
\citet{li_scheduling_location_2022} use processing and release times to bound the makespan and strengthen cuts by identifying smaller sets of assignments that cannot improve the incumbent.
Similarly, feasibility cuts can be strengthened by removing assignments and re-solving the subproblem to identify a smaller set that remains infeasible \citep{elci_stochastic_2022}.
For mixed-integer recourse, lower approximations of recourse costs can also support optimality cuts \citep{vanderlaan_converging_2024,baals_milk_runs_2026}.
Monotonicity is another property that can extend the information obtained from a subproblem evaluation to other master solutions.
Examples include cuts based on monotone route recourse costs and on simulation evaluations of resource allocations \citep{parada_disaggregated_2024,forbes_simulation_2024}.

When subproblems are expensive to solve, relaxations can provide information to the master at lower computational cost.
LP relaxations of integer recourse, for example, can generate cuts and eliminate some candidate solutions before integer subproblems are solved \citep{angulo_improving_2016,satici_network_2026}.
\citet{calik_stations_2019} solve a partially relaxed model and check whether its optimal value can be attained by an integer solution with the same first-stage decisions.
If not, they continue the search and add combinatorial feasibility cuts for integer-infeasible subproblems.
Relaxing coupling constraints can also simplify the master problem, but a feasible solution must ultimately satisfy those constraints.
\citet{chen_integrated_2023} relax the nonanticipativity of the initial vehicle allocation, allowing each scenario to choose its own allocation, and also relax vehicle-flow integrality.
They then restore a common allocation and use a diving heuristic to construct integer operational solutions.
Thus, relaxed solutions can support both cut generation and feasible-solution search.

Existing studies have developed reformulations, valid inequalities, strengthened cuts, and relaxation-based procedures to improve Benders decomposition, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:lit-method-components}.
In our problem, fixing the DWC deployment leaves a recourse problem in each scenario that selects battery capacities and charging schedules.
The limited capacity of plug-in stations couples charging decisions across routes, so each scenario subproblem remains a large mixed-integer program.
In the natural decomposition, the master does not explicitly represent these battery and charging decisions and must learn their feasibility and cost through cuts.
Repeatedly solving the coupled scenario subproblems can therefore be computationally expensive.
These difficulties motivate a decomposition method that strengthens the master's representation of recourse and exploits route structure to reduce the burden of scenario evaluation.
